A Deep Q-Learning agent for trading

I taught a neural network, built from scratch in NumPy, to decide when to be long and when to stay out of the market. Here's how I built it, what metrics I used to judge it, and what happened when I tested it against simply buying and holding.

D. Quispe · September 2026

The idea is simple to state and much less simple to pull off: on every trading day, the agent chooses between two positions -long or flat- trying to beat the most boring, hardest-to-beat strategy there is: staying long the whole time (buy & hold). This wasn't an exercise in "I built a model and it made money" -in fact, in two of the three cases I tested, it lost to the benchmark- but rather in honestly documenting what worked, what didn't, and why.

The environment: what the agent sees, and how it gets paid

It all starts with a series of closing prices \(P = (p_0, p_1, \dots, p_T)\). From that, the daily return:

(1) \[ r_t = \frac{p_{t+1} - p_t}{p_t} \]
\(r_t\)
percentage return from day \(t\) to \(t+1\).
\(p_t\)
closing price on day \(t\).

Plainly: "what percentage did the price go up or down from one day to the next?" - the change \((p_{t+1}-p_t)\) expressed as a fraction of the starting price, so the number is comparable across assets of different value.

The agent can only take two actions, \(a_t \in \{0, 1\}\): \(0\) is flat, \(1\) is long. What it "sees" at each moment isn't the full price history, but a window of the last \(w\) returns plus its current position:

(2) \[ s_t = \big(\, r_{t-w}, \, r_{t-w+1}, \, \dots, \, r_{t-1}, \, a_{t-1} \,\big) \in \mathbb{R}^{w+1} \]
\(s_t\)
the only thing the agent "sees" to decide at instant \(t\).
\(w\)
window size (\(w=10\) in my tests).
\(a_{t-1}\)
whether it was already long or not, one step before.

Plainly: a small "window" of the last \(w\) days, plus one piece of memory (was it already in?). \(\mathbb{R}^{w+1}\) is just notation for "a vector of \(w+1\) real numbers."

And the reward: if it's long, it pockets the day's return; if it switches position, it pays a transaction cost.

(3) \[ R_t = a_t \cdot r_t \; - \; c \cdot \mathbb{1}[a_t \neq a_{t-1}] \]
\(R_t\)
reward at step \(t\).
\(c\)
cost of switching position (\(c=0.0005\), 5 basis points).
\(\mathbb{1}[\cdot]\)
1 if the condition is true, 0 otherwise.

Plainly: if it's long (\(a_t=1\)) it keeps \(r_t\); if it's flat, that term is zero. The second term only kicks in on the day it switches position relative to the day before -the cost of entering or exiting.

Across every test I used \(w = 10\) and \(c = 0.0005\).

The network, and how it trains

No PyTorch or TensorFlow here -the network that approximates \(Q(s,a)\) I wrote by hand, a multilayer perceptron with forward and backward propagation implemented from scratch. Given a list of layer sizes \((n_0, \dots, n_L)\), every layer does the same thing:

(4) \[ z^{(i)} = a^{(i-1)} W_i + b_i, \qquad a^{(i)} = \begin{cases} \tanh(z^{(i)}) & i < L \\ z^{(i)} & i = L \end{cases} \]
\(z^{(i)}\)
linear output of layer \(i\), before activation.
\(a^{(i)}\)
output after activation; feeds into layer \(i+1\).
\(W_i, b_i\)
the parameters that get adjusted during training.
\(L\)
total number of layers (\(L=2\): one hidden; \(L=4\): three hidden).

Plainly: multiply by weights, add a bias, bend it with \(\tanh\), and pass the result to the next layer. The last layer is left unbent because Q-values can be any real number, not just \((-1,1)\).

I tried two depths: one hidden layer of 64 units, and three hidden layers of 64 each. For training I used standard DQN -a target network synced every 10 episodes (no averaging, a direct weight copy), and a replay buffer from which minibatches are sampled so training isn't done on correlated transitions. The training target for a transition \((s, a, r, s')\):

(5) \[ y = r + \gamma \max_{a'} Q_{\theta^-}(s', a') \]
\(y\)
what the network "should" have predicted.
\(\gamma\)
discount factor (\(\gamma=0.99\)): how much the future is worth.
\(Q_{\theta^-}\)
the target network, a frozen copy of the main one.

Plainly: the real reward plus an estimate of how much the best possible future is worth. The frozen network (not the one being updated) is used to estimate that future, because otherwise the target keeps moving and never converges.

Weights are updated by minimizing \(\mathcal{L} = (Q_\theta(s,a) - y)^2\) with manual backpropagation, layer by layer, using Adam as the optimizer.

Exploration

An \(\varepsilon\)-greedy policy with exponential decay:

(6) \[ \varepsilon(e) = \max\big(\varepsilon_{\min},\; \varepsilon_0 \cdot \rho^{\,e}\big) \]
\(\varepsilon(e)\)
probability of acting randomly in episode \(e\).
\(\varepsilon_0, \varepsilon_{\min}\)
initial value (1.0) and floor (0.05).
\(\rho\)
decay rate (\(\rho=0.995\)).

Plainly: at first it always explores (100% random). As episodes go by, it trusts what it's learned more and more. The floor keeps it from ever stopping exploring entirely and getting stuck in a bad policy.

How I judge it

Four standard metrics, computed on the equity curve \(E = (E_0, \dots, E_n)\) with \(E_0 = 1\):

Total return -gain or loss from start to finish:

(7) \[ \text{RT} = \frac{E_n - E_0}{E_0} \]

Sharpe ratio (annualized) -return per unit of risk taken:

(8) \[ \text{Sharpe} = \frac{\bar{r}}{\sigma_r} \sqrt{252} \]

Plainly: two strategies can have the same average return, but if one gets there with much sharper swings, it's "worse" in risk terms. Sharpe divides by that volatility; \(\sqrt{252}\) just rescales it to an annual basis.

Max drawdown -the worst drop from a historical peak:

(9) \[ \text{MDD} = \min_t \left( \frac{E_t - \max_{i \le t} E_i}{\max_{i \le t} E_i} \right) \]

Plainly: day by day, "how much have I lost since my best moment so far?" Max drawdown is the worst of those drops across the whole period -the most painful stretch, even if it recovers afterward.

Win rate -share of winning trades:

(10) \[ \text{WR} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N} \mathbb{1}\!\left[\prod_{t \in \text{trade}_k}(1+r_t) - 1 > 0\right] \]

Plainly: the daily returns within a single trade multiply together (compounding), they don't add up. If that trade's compounded result is positive, it counts as a win.

For every scenario I split the series 80/20 (train/test), with the agent never seeing the test data during training. 300 episodes, target network synced every 10.

Was it the market, or was it the network?

With two real scenarios losing to the benchmark, the question remained: is the agent structurally at a disadvantage by stepping out of the market during uptrends, or does the single-layer network simply not have enough capacity to pick up the signal in real data? I repeated the same training and backtest on MU and AAPL, changing nothing else, only swapping the architecture for three hidden layers.

There was the answer, and it was fairly clear: on both real assets, the deeper network improved all four metrics without exception. The most interesting part is that the deep agent's Sharpe ratio beat buy & hold's on both (2.894 vs. 2.774 on MU; 1.503 vs. 1.064 on AAPL) -something that hadn't happened once with the shallow network. Much of what looked like "the market beats the agent" was actually "the network didn't have enough left to give."

Notice what didn't change: the agent's absolute total return is still below buy & hold on both assets, even with the deep network. The improvement is in the risk-return relationship and in drawdown control, not in capturing more raw return during the climb. If what matters is the final number in the account, buy & hold still wins. If what matters is how much risk you took to get there, the deep agent now competes on equal footing.

What I take away from this

First: the agent's performance against simply staying long depends a lot on the regime of whatever period it's tested on. In the synthetic case, with genuine downtrends, it could squeeze value out of dodging them. In both real cases, the test period coincided with sustained bull markets -there, any strategy that steps out of the market starts at a structural disadvantage.

But that reading gets strongly qualified by the network-depth result: a good part of what initially looked like "the agent can't compete in real markets" was, more precisely, "the single-layer network couldn't represent the policy it needed to." A problem with the environment isn't the same as a problem with the model's capacity, and it's worth ruling out the second before drawing conclusions about the first.

Something else I noticed: MU's training curve with the small network converged much more cleanly than AAPL's, and yet the agent still ended up losing to the benchmark in both cases with that architecture. A healthy training curve doesn't guarantee that the learned policy beats what you're comparing it to -those are two different things.

And the limitations, stated plainly: I trained for 300 episodes, which is low for DQN; I used a single train/test split per scenario, with no walk-forward validation, so I can't cleanly separate genuine skill from regime luck; the action space is binary (long/flat), with no short positions; and each configuration ran with a single seed, so part of the improvement from going deeper could be due to initialization rather than model capacity alone. With two real assets and one synthetic series, there's also no basis to generalize much further than what I saw here.

With all of that on the table, here's what I'm keeping: going from "the agent loses" to "the agent competes on risk-return, with a better architecture" was the most useful part of the whole exercise -and it's a good habit in general, before blaming the market, to first rule out that the model had more to give.
